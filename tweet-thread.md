## Variant A — the argument first

1. Every AI-designed page looks like every other AI-designed page. That's not the model being bad at design. That's what happens when everyone lets the same model decide what "good" means.

2. We're all doing something ambitious and can't think through every choice ourselves, so the model fills the gaps with its own taste. Same taste, everyone. Readers learn the pattern fast. That sameness is what gets called AI slop.

3. What the model knows about you can be poison or antidote. The right context pulls the work toward you. The same context, left running, locks you into your own rut.

4. My fix: dice. I wrote the most boring brief I could — a one-page weekly website report, fake data — and made the model fold in two random ingredients. Cthulhu × Fast food. Bureaucracy × Disco. Deep-sea bioluminescence × Victorian insect collecting.

5. Then the same harsh critique prompt, three times in a row: commit harder to both ingredients, keep the report readable. Every attempt is saved, including the passes where it looked at its own page and changed nothing. All the prompts are inspectable.

   https://randomness-for-style.vercel.app/

6. Some passes get better. Some just get louder. Almost none of them look like The Template, which was the point. If I'm going to ship slop, at least it's my own slop.

7. New this week: the whole experiment rerun on a model one generation newer, identical prompts, dice and all. Flip between generations on the page and judge for yourself whether taste converged.

   https://randomness-for-style.vercel.app/?model=newer

## Variant B — the demo first

1. I built a slot machine for taste. Two dice, 36 combinations, one deliberately boring weekly report. The dice land on Cthulhu × Fast food and the model has to design around whatever came up.

   https://randomness-for-style.vercel.app/

2. The control group is the same report with no dice: tidy, sensible, forgettable. Everyone who asks a model for a report gets roughly this page, because the model is applying its own definition of good. That sameness is what people call AI slop.

3. The dice make a choice the model would never make for you, and the brief forces it to commit. Bureaucracy × Disco comes back as a performance permit. Motorsport × Opera is a programme in three acts. None of it is The Template.

4. Then the same harsh critique prompt, three times: does it develop the idea, or just add decoration? Both happen, and both are saved — unchanged passes included, prompts and critiques open for inspection.

5. The general point: what the model knows about you is poison or antidote. Context can pull the work toward you, or lock you into your own rut. Randomness is the cheap way out of the rut. The model is already non-deterministic; add some randomness of your own.

6. Also on the page now: the identical experiment rerun on a newer model, so you can check whether a year of progress made the taste converge or diverge. https://randomness-for-style.vercel.app/?model=newer

## Alternate hooks (swap into any variant)

- The problem with letting AI make your choices isn't that its taste is bad. It's that its taste is identical for everyone who asks.
- I made a model design a weekly report as Cthulhu × Fast food, then told it three times it wasn't committing hard enough. For science.
- AI slop is not a quality problem. It's a recognition problem: we've all seen the template before.
- Your context window is poison or antidote. It can pull the model toward you, or wall you into the version of you it already knows.

## Alternate closers

- The dice don't make the work good. They make it yours to judge. That turns out to be most of the job.
- None of this proves randomness makes work better. It reliably makes it less recognizable, and recognizable is the problem.

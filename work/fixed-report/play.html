AI design has a look, and most of us can now spot it. So one report was made 36 times, each with two ingredients nobody would pair on purpose. The numbers never change. Only the dice do.

  
About the experiment
A lot of what we call AI slop is just repetition, aka pattern recognition. We’re all working with the same colleague (Claude, Codex). We’re seeing more work, and more of the same work. My colleagues wouldn’t call my work Mike slop unless my work was absolutely everywhere and they were seeing the lower-effort end of it. A way around this is randomness. Not always better, but definitely different. This is my approach, and this experiment is my attempt to prove it. Models will change, so will weights, and so will how much randomness shows between one piece of work and the next. But adding more randomness will always scale, though again, not always for the better.
The brief never changes: one weekly report, the same numbers. After the dice, the model is told three times to criticise its page and try again. The page you see is the try worth keeping, and it is rarely the last. Show every pass brings back the rest.
Nothing is generated live. Every page was saved from a real run, and every run used two ingredients. So after your first throw the page already borrows the face the second die is showing, named in the dashed gap. The stronger prompt starts over from the same first draft. The newer model repeats the whole thing a generation later. More passes do not always make a better page.
